value: DON BOURBON'S — The Officially Unofficial History of a Very Important Man

Nobody remembers exactly when Don Bourbon became Don Bourbon. Some insist it happened on a golf course. Others claim it was after the sixth glass of bourbon. One unreliable witness maintains that he was born wearing a golf glove and requesting a tee time.

What everyone agrees upon is that Don Bourbon never intended to found a kingdom. He simply wanted to play golf. Unfortunately, a kingdom formed around him anyway.

First came Pretty Boy Vince, the Prince of Obedience, with a Colgate smile and a remarkable talent for polishing a throne nobody had built. Then Butcher Boy, carrying an imaginary axe and enough confidence to make a medieval executioner reconsider his career. Somewhere in the distance, an engine started: Driver for Schneider. Nobody knew where he was going, or why Schneider needed driving. The car was running, and apparently that was reason enough.

Together they became the Three Musketeers of J-LAND. Three for one, and one for Don.

Don Bourbon had loyal subjects, a magnificent court, endless opinions, and an almost supernatural ability to disappear whenever a golf course called his name. There was only one problem: his kingdom had no capital.

And so Don Bourbon's was born. An oak bar for royal deliberations. Leather chairs for gentlemen who had accomplished nothing but required immediate rest. A crooked stage for speeches nobody requested. Above the fireplace hung a portrait of Don Bourbon raising a glass to his greatest achievement: having absolutely no idea he had become the main character of an entire musical.

Golf clubs crossed like ceremonial swords. Forgotten tournaments were whispered with religious reverence. A velvet curtain concealed the stage where Golferia might someday be performed.

One evening, a golden crab appeared on the bar. Nobody admitted bringing it. Some said courage. Others said immortality. The bartender said poor judgment. The crab became the house's guardian.

And the establishment acquired its only sacred rule: No man is so important that he cannot be laughed at.

Today Don Bourbon's welcomes kings without kingdoms, princes without qualifications, heroes of questionable achievements, and anyone who has ever made a terrible decision with magnificent confidence. There are no royal titles except the ones we invent. No compulsory speeches except the ones we interrupt. And one question the bartender will never ask: 'Shouldn't you be on the golf course?' Because somewhere, somehow, Don Bourbon already is.

Where every legend is exaggerated, every throne is slightly crooked, and nobody gets the last word.
